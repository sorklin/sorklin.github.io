Cyber Risk Quantification

If you can't express it in dollars, you're still guessing.

“High risk, red box” is not a decision — it's a colour someone picked on a Tuesday. I spent years building probabilistic models of what could actually go wrong, running them tens of thousands of times and handing over the distribution. Then I helped build the product that does it faster, so the argument can be about numbers instead of adjectives — in rooms I'm not always standing in. A decade of probabilistic cyber risk models — named scenarios, calibrated estimates and Monte Carlo simulation — reported in financial terms that support funding, insurance and board decisions. That practice is now built into a platform other practitioners run themselves.

How the number gets built → How it became a product

What changes

Same risks. Different sentences.

The findings don't change much. What changes is whether anyone can act on them.

What the report usually saysWhat a quantified model says
“Ransomware: High risk”“Ransomware: $1.8M expected annual loss, $6.4M at the 95th percentile.”
“Improve third-party assurance”“The backup vendor drives 38% of modeled loss; tightening that contract is the cheapest curve movement available.”
“We need more security budget”“These three controls cost $210k and remove $1.1M of expected annual loss. Here is the curve both ways.”
“Our premium went up 30%”“The premium is 2.1× the modeled retained loss above your deductible. Here's what to negotiate with.”
“We're compliant”“Compliant, and still exposed at 1-in-8 for the scenario that actually keeps you awake. Both facts are in the table.”

The work itself

Not a workshop about feelings.

  • Scenario construction. Named, bounded, plausible loss events — ransomware, BEC, cloud misconfiguration, third-party outage, data spill — each with a cost anatomy.
  • Calibration. Getting ranges out of the people who actually run the systems, with enough scepticism built in that the numbers survive the next audit.
  • Simulation. Monte Carlo over those ranges: expected annual loss, percentile points, and the tail where the real arguments live.
  • Control ROI. Which mitigations move which part of the curve, and what they cost to get there.
  • Insurance alignment. Modeled loss versus your limits, retention and premium — so the renewal conversation has arithmetic in it.
  • Board readout. One page, one curve, three decisions. Long version stays in the appendix where it belongs.

Deliverables

  • Quantified scenario register, owned and re-runnable
  • Portfolio and per-scenario exceedance curves
  • Ranked control set with cost / curve-movement pairs
  • Executive page + defensible model backing
  • A refresh cadence, because a 2024 number is a 2024 weather report

Every model is wrong. The job is to make yours wrong in a way that's useful — and, ideally, cheaper than the incident it's predicting.

Where the work happens

Axio360 — where the practice became a product.

I do product management at Axio and head the quantitative risk modelling team: VP of Product Management, Quantification Modeling, and product owner of the quantification capability inside Axio360. Which means I decide what the platform does with an assessment, a scenario, a model and the insurance conversation that follows — a decision engine rather than four spreadsheets with four different owners. There was a stretch when this was me, a spreadsheet and a week. It is now a team's work — product management, modelling, engineering — that lets any practitioner skip both.

The job, honestly described

  • Owning the quantification capability — the roadmap, the models, and the definition of good — settled before any of it ships.
  • Assessments against the models that matter — NIST CSF 2.0, C2M2, CIS 18 — so the control baseline is evidence, not opinion.
  • Scenario collections built from the catalog and shaped to the actual environment, with susceptibility set by people who know the estate.
  • Estimated values under the hood — server counts, forensic rates, dwell and recovery time — because a curve is only as good as the numbers feeding it.
  • Catalogs and bespoke models — industry-specific and AI-curated content, plus the modelling team I head, which builds the ones no catalog covers.
  • Impact distributions & exceedance curves, per scenario and across the whole collection.
  • Quadrant breakdowns: first-party versus third-party, financial versus physical. Most organisations discover their biggest exposure is in the quadrant they weren't funding.
  • Assessment ↔ scenario linkage, so a control gap traces to a number and a number traces back to a gap.

Ground rules

What this is not.

  • A 5×5 heat map with a red box in the corner. I will not be drawing one.
  • A fear-and-uncertainty sales deck for a product I don't sell.
  • A compliance exercise. Compliance is a floor, occasionally a ceiling; it is rarely a risk profile.
  • A false-precision parade. You'll get ranges and percentiles, not “$4,382,911.62”.

How it goes

The method, step by step.

Kick-off & asset scoping

One call, one list: what would genuinely hurt, and what it costs per day.

Calibration sessions

Structured estimation with the people who own the systems. Short, explicit, occasionally argumentative.

Model & simulate

Scenarios built and run in Axio360; sensitivities checked before anyone sees a headline.

Readout

The curve, the movement options, and what I'd fund if it were my company — which, structurally, it is.
